1. Matrix Properties and Concepts

1.6 As mentioned in the text, the ovals of Cassini, which first appeared in

Brauer (1946), provide an eigenvalue inclusion region for a given n x n
matrix A, and, as mentioned in (1.50) of Theorem 1.24, the union of
the Cassini ovals can be no worse (and are often much better) than
the associated eigenvalue inclusion region provided by the Gerschgorin
disks for this matrix. (See also Varga and Krautstengl (1999), which
includes an interesting interactive Supplement where, on selecting any
3 x 3 complex matrix of your choice, the eigenvalues of this matrix, its
associated Gerschgorin disks, and its associated ovals of Cassini are all
produced in color!)

It is also interesting to mention that Brauer (1947) stated, in analogy
with Taussky’s Theorem 1.18, that given any irreducible n xn complex
matrix A with n > 2 and given any eigenvalue \ of A which lies on
the boundary of the union of the n(n — 1)/2 ovals of Cassini, then all
the ovals of Cassini pass through A, i.e., with the notations of (1.33),
ie.,

(1.59) |A — aya] -|A — aj,3| = AiA; for alli Aj, 1<i,j<n.

This is true for n = 2, but it turns out that this long-standing result
is false in general for all n > 3. This was only recently pointed out by
Zhang and Gu (1994), who also give a proper formulation of (1.59),
based on circuits in the directed graph of A. See also Brualdi (1982)
for other interesting connections with ovals of Cassini.
